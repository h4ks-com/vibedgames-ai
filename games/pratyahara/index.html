the board

—

what this is, and why it is shaped like this

Pāṇini opens the Aṣṭādhyāyī with fourteen short sūtras that list every sound of Sanskrit in a particular order. The order is the point. Take any sound in the list and any of the fourteen anubandhas — the marker consonants that close each sūtra and are not themselves sounds of the language — and the pair denotes everything lying between them. That is ādir antyena sahetā (A 1.1.71), and it is the only thing you do to the board here: click an initial, click a marker, and you have made a pratyāhāra, a name for a set of sounds that no other notation of the ancient world could express as briefly.

The rest of the grammar is then written in those names. iko yaṇ aci is seven syllables and it says: the vowels i u ṛ ḷ become the semivowels y v r l when a vowel follows. You cannot state the rule until you hold the three pratyāhāras, and you cannot hold those until you know the sūtras they are cut from. That dependency is real, and it is what this game is made of — nothing is gated by an arbitrary price, only by what the next thing is actually built out of.

The economy is the one argued for in plate six. What you own produces only while you can still retrieve it: everything here decays, and reciting is what restores it. A correct answer always pays the same amount, because a reward you cannot predict is a slot machine; the question is what varies. Nothing is ever lost for a wrong answer beyond the review it costs you.

Order of study is the traditional one — varṇasamāmnāya, then the saṃjñā that name what the sounds do, then vidhi, the rules that operate. Sūtra numbers are given so that any of it can be looked up in Pāṇini.
