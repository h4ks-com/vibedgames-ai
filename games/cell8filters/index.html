What runs. The picture is laid on a hexagonal grid, one colour to a cell. A filter is a rule that gives each cell a new colour from its own colour and those of its six neighbours, applied to every cell at once, a set number of times. Many filters are two or three such rules one after another. Nothing else is used: no rule knows where its cell is or what the picture looks like far away.

The state of a cell is three bytes of colour and one helper byte. The helper byte is a private source of chance (each cell mixes its own with its neighbours' at every step), and in Crystallize it records which cells have been claimed. The port, the eighth input of Cell-8, is used here as a mask: with "Leave untouched" set, cells of that tone are at port 0 and keep their colour.

How faithful these are. Each filter was written from what the original is known to do, in whole-number arithmetic on seven cells. None was compared with the original's output. Those marked "approximation" below copy the idea of a filter whose exact method is different or unpublished. A step reaches one cell further, so a radius of n takes n steps.

What cannot be done this way, and why, is listed in the table. The usual reason is that the filter needs to know where a cell is in the picture (twirls, tiles, halftone screens, flares), which a cellular automaton cannot.
